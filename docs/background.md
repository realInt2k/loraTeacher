# Background

This page summarizes the work our project builds on, in the order in which it matters to us. It is meant as a starting point for the related-work section of the report. Values attributed to a paper were taken from the paper text where possible; values read off a figure are marked as approximate.

## The original result

Cloud et al. (2025, arXiv:2507.14805, later published in Nature) introduced subliminal learning. A teacher model is given a trait, usually through a system prompt such as "You love owls", and asked to continue lists of numbers. The answers are filtered so that only plain number lists remain. A student that starts from the same base model and is fine-tuned on these lists picks up the trait. Most of their experiments use GPT-4.1 nano, where the effect is strong for several animals and trees and even for misalignment. On the open model Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct the effect appears for only a few animals, such as cat, penguin and phoenix. Transfer does not work between different base models, which the authors take as a sign that the hidden signal is specific to the model's own parameters. In a side experiment they also fine-tune a teacher on the evaluation questions instead of prompting it, and report a similar effect, but without any control over how that teacher was fine-tuned.

They also give a theoretical argument. If a teacher and a student start from exactly the same parameters, and the teacher takes one small gradient step towards a trait, then a student trained by one gradient step to imitate the teacher's outputs moves in a direction that does not point away from the teacher's change, whatever the training inputs are. The authors themselves note that their experiments break these assumptions, since they use many steps, sampled outputs instead of full output distributions, and filtering. For our project the important point is that with LoRA the teacher and student do not start from exactly the same parameters, because each adapter begins from its own random matrix.

## The role of LoRA rank and context

Nief et al. (2026, arXiv:2606.00831) ran a very large number of experiments on Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct and Gemma-3-4B-it with prompted teachers, and found that the effect depends on the rank of the student's LoRA adapter in an inverted-U shape: it is weak at very low rank, strongest at some middle rank, and gone with very high rank or full fine-tuning. Cat peaks at rank 8, wolf at 32 and eagle at 128. Looking across all 21 animals in their figures, about half show such a curve, around six show nothing, and panda behaves differently because it is already the model's favorite. They report only the rate of the target animal, not what happens to the other answers. They also show that the effect largely disappears when the system prompt at evaluation differs from the one used in training. Their settings are the ones most of the field now follows: LoRA with alpha equal to the rank, AdamW with learning rate 2e-4, three epochs and an effective batch of 66.

Feng (2026, LessWrong) revisits the rank curve and argues that much of it comes from using one learning rate for all ranks. When the learning rate is tuned for each rank, transfer happens at every rank, and higher ranks mainly need more training data. This matters for how we describe our student-rank results, since we also use a fixed learning rate.

## Explanations and fine-tuned teachers

Blank et al. (2026, arXiv:2606.00995) explain subliminal learning as the distillation of a steering vector. The trait prompt acts on the teacher roughly like adding a fixed direction to its internal activations, and the student learns a direction that is aligned with it. In an appendix they also test one fine-tuned cat teacher and find weaker transfer than with a prompted teacher, though the teacher's rank and strength are not reported. Schrodi et al. (2026, ICLR) show that the signal is concentrated in a small set of positions where a biased and an unbiased teacher would choose different tokens, which they call divergence tokens, and that early layers are especially important. They also note that Qwen students sometimes answer "qwen" because of the default system prompt.

Several recent papers use fine-tuned teachers. Vo et al. (2026, arXiv:2609.25721) pass a trait through ten generations of LoRA fine-tuned models and note that the effect of the teacher's rank has not been studied; in their two attempts with larger teacher adapters, the teachers started mentioning the trait in their number answers and transfer dropped. Tang et al. (2026, arXiv:2610.30884) train over a hundred small fine-tuned teachers and find that a teacher's own strength is the best predictor of how much it transmits. Dubiński et al. (2026, arXiv:2610.10657) show that capabilities and backdoors can also be transmitted, and that transfer is stronger when teacher and student use the same kind of LoRA adapter.

## LoRA itself

LoRA (Hu et al., 2022) fine-tunes a model by adding to each weight matrix a product of two thin matrices, so that only a small number of parameters is trained. The rank is the width of these matrices, and a scaling factor alpha divided by the rank controls the size of the update. Shuttleworth et al. (2024, arXiv:2410.21228) show that LoRA updates contain new directions that are almost orthogonal to the pre-trained weights, which they call intruder dimensions, and that this depends on rank and alpha. This suggests that weight similarity between two LoRA adapters may be dominated by such directions, which is why our alignment analysis compares against baselines.

## Gaps our project addresses

From this literature, three questions are open. Nobody has varied the LoRA rank of a fine-tuned teacher in a controlled way, nor looked at how teacher rank interacts with student rank. Nobody has compared a LoRA teacher's weight change with its student's, although activation-level alignment has been studied. And earlier rank studies report only the target animal, so shifts in the rest of the answer distribution, such as the panda effect, have gone unexamined.
